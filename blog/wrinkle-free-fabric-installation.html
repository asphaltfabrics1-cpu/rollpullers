5 Proven Techniques for Wrinkle-Free Fabric Installation

Wrinkles cost time and rework. When a crease shows up under the tack coat, you're looking at cut-outs, re-tacks, and a foreman making excuses to the inspector. Here's how experienced crews prevent creases before the brush ever touches down.

Why Wrinkles Happen in the First Place

A wrinkle in paving fabric is almost never the fabric's fault. It's a symptom of uneven tension, a brush that's not tracking, a roll that's spinning faster than the tractor is moving, or an operator trying to correct all three at once. Fix the root causes and the fabric goes down flat the first time.

The five techniques below cover the equipment settings and field habits that separate clean installs from callback jobs. None of them require exotic hardware—most are standard capabilities on the Grizzly 600T and Cub 300T that operators simply need to dial in.

1. Dial in Dual-PVC Tension Before You Start the Pass

The patented dual-bar telescoping PVC tensioning system on the Grizzly is designed to double-tension the material as it leaves the roll. Six sliding brackets let you distribute stretch evenly across the roll width so the fabric arrives at the brush already flat and obedient.

Set tension before you roll forward, not after. If you wait until the fabric is already unrolling to tighten things up, you'll chase creases the entire pass. Match slider position to roll width and stiffness—wider or slicker materials need more aggressive pinning on the radius plate (anywhere from 0° to 45°) to hold their shape.

  • Preset sliders to the roll's advertised width, then fine-tune the first 20 feet
  • For grids and hybrid mats, start at higher pin angles and back off if the material stretches too far
  • Recheck tension after every roll change—cores are not identical between batches

Reading Tension Visually

A properly tensioned sheet hangs with a light, uniform curve as it approaches the brush. Slack loops, fluttering edges, or one side visibly tighter than the other are all red flags. Stop, adjust, and continue—don't try to brush your way through it.

2. Let the Articulating Brush System Follow the Crown

The articulating center brush mount is engineered to pivot with road crown and surface variation. Dual leveling jacks let you preset the brush contact pressure, and the four-position articulation range keeps polypropylene filaments engaged across the full sweep width—from 6'10" to 13 feet with telescoping angled brushes, and beyond 18 feet with extensions.

Common mistake: locking the brush too rigid to save adjustment time. That leaves the outer edges hovering above a crowned road, and any air gap under the brush becomes a wrinkle waiting to happen. Loosen the leveling jacks a quarter-turn if you're seeing edge-only creases—it's almost always contact pressure, not fabric quality.

3. Set the Roll Brake to Match the Tractor's Speed

The rotating spindle roll holders on the Grizzly use disc brakes to modulate roll speed. If the brake is too loose, the roll spins ahead of the tractor and fabric bunches at the brush. Too tight, and the roll drags—stretching the sheet unevenly and telegraphing waves down the pass.

The rule of thumb: brake pressure should just barely hold the roll from freewheeling when the tractor stops. Anything more is friction you don't want. On curves, the inside edge naturally travels slower than the outside, so a well-balanced brake lets the disc mechanism absorb that differential without operator input.

  • Test brake feel with the tractor stationary before starting the pass
  • Recalibrate after cold mornings—brake pad tension changes with temperature
  • Replace pads when you notice the roll "catching" or slipping unpredictably

4. Position Telescoping Arms So the Brush Ends Flush with the Fabric Edge

Telescoping arms on bearings let you extend or retract width smoothly, but the goal isn't just to match roll width—it's to make sure the brush ends land exactly at the fabric edge. Overshoot and the outer filaments drag on tack coat, kicking up asphalt emulsion. Undershoot and the fabric edge curls up unbrushed.

Take the extra minute to sight down the arm and confirm the brush tip aligns with the fabric selvage. On telescoping Grizzly 600T frames with the optional hydraulic kit, this adjustment happens on the fly from the tractor seat. On mechanical setups, lock the arm before you commit to the pass.

Working Near Curbs and Guardrails

For work within six inches of a curb, deploy the crab-claw swing arms. The mechanical (or hydraulic-optional) swing lets the brush finish the edge without repositioning the tractor—which is where a lot of wrinkles get born, since every stop-and-restart creates a tension discontinuity in the fabric behind you.

5. Manage Overlaps and Roll Changes Deliberately

Even a perfectly tensioned pass can wrinkle at the seam if the overlap is sloppy. Standard practice is a 4- to 6-inch longitudinal overlap and 12-inch transverse overlap, both brushed down immediately after placement. The operator on the ground should walk the seam behind the brush to catch any lift-off before it sets.

Roll changes are the other high-risk moment. When the outgoing roll is spent, the fabric loses tension for a few seconds—which is enough to create a ripple if the brush is still pressing. Coordinate the tractor pause with the roll swap, and don't resume forward motion until the new roll is threaded, tensioned, and the brush is back in position.

  • Mark overlap targets on the ground before the pass so the driver has a visual
  • Brush transverse seams by hand if the equipment brush can't reach cleanly
  • Never resume forward motion with a slack roll—wait for tension to reset

Troubleshooting Common Wrinkle Patterns

Different wrinkle patterns point to different root causes. Learning to read them cuts your diagnostic time in half:

  • Center creases running with the direction of travel: brush articulation locked too tight, or center mount not following crown
  • Edge waves on one side only: unequal tension across the roll—rebalance the PVC bracket sliders
  • Transverse ripples every few feet: roll brake grabbing intermittently, or tractor speed inconsistent
  • Accordion folds at the brush: tension too loose, or brush contact pressure too high
  • Bunching at roll changes: forward motion started before the new roll tensioned
