Extending Equipment Life: Maintenance Schedules That Work

Preventive maintenance isn't glamorous, but it's why nearly every Grizzly ever built is still in service. A machine that gets 15 minutes of attention at the end of each shift outlives a machine that gets ignored until something breaks— by decades, not years.

Why Grizzlies Last So Long

The one-piece welded frame, the disc-braked rotating spindles, the polypropylene brushes with a high melt rating—these design choices exist specifically to reduce what can go wrong in the field. But even the toughest platform benefits from consistent attention, and the crews that treat maintenance as a discipline get the most out of their investment.

Nearly every Grizzly ever built is still operating somewhere in the world. That's not marketing—it's the natural result of purpose-built engineering plus operators who follow a schedule. The maintenance intervals below are what those long-lived machines have in common.

Daily: End-of-Shift Walk-Around

Fifteen minutes at the end of each shift catches 90 percent of the problems that would otherwise become tomorrow's downtime. This isn't a deep inspection—it's a consistent walk-around with a rag, a grease gun, and a flashlight.

  • Wipe down tack coat and asphalt residue from telescoping arms and PVC brackets before it hardens
  • Grease all fittings—swing-arm pivots, telescoping tube joints, spindle bearings
  • Check disc brake pads for wear and confirm brake tension holds when the roll is manually spun
  • Inspect brush filaments for melted tips, matted sections, or missing blocks
  • Look for loose bolts on the mainframe, brush brackets, and swing-arm mounts
  • Confirm hydraulic hoses (if equipped) show no chafing or seepage

The tack coat cleanup step matters more than it sounds. Emulsion that hardens on telescoping tubes creates friction that slows adjustments and eventually damages the bearing surfaces. A quick wipe with the right solvent while it's still soft saves hours of scraping later.

Weekly: Deeper Inspection

Once a week—typically at the end of the workweek—give the machine a more thorough look. This is when you catch issues that daily walk-arounds miss.

  • Rotate and inspect all brush blocks; swap positions to even out wear
  • Torque-check mainframe bolts and universal mount hardware
  • Inspect solid steel cones for wear; sharpen if grip on rough roads has degraded
  • Check spindle disc brake pads more carefully; replace if approaching wear limits
  • Verify telescoping arm bearings move smoothly—no gritty feel or binding
  • Inspect PVC bracket sliders and radius plates for wear at pin positions

Brush Replacement Timing

Brushes are consumables, but they don't fail on a fixed schedule—they fail based on how many square yards you've pushed through them and the temperature range they've been exposed to. The polypropylene extra-fill brushes have a melt rating around 325°F, which handles most tack coat temperatures without degradation, but hot summer overlays can still soften the filament tips.

Replace brush blocks when:

  • Filaments have visibly melted or fused into clumps
  • Coverage has thinned to the point where fabric shows brush track marks
  • Blocks show cracking or hardware slippage in the threaded inserts
  • You've completed a heavy season and want to start fresh for the next one

Ordering brushes in advance of the season—not the day you need them—is the cheapest form of insurance in this business.

Monthly: Component-Level Attention

Monthly maintenance addresses the components that don't fail catastrophically but degrade gradually. Skip these and you'll pay for it in rebuild costs a few years down the road.

  • Full spindle assembly inspection—bearings, brake pads, T-screws, neoprene seals
  • Telescoping tube bearing and seal package check; replace worn seals before dirt intrudes
  • Hydraulic system check (if equipped)—fluid level, filter condition, cylinder rod inspection
  • Swing-arm pivot inspection for play or wear at the mechanical or hydraulic joints
  • Universal mount bracket check for elongated pin holes or bushing wear

Spindle Rebuilds

Rotating spindle roll holders are the heart of the tension system, and they take the most consistent load of any component on the machine. GAC offers rebuild kits that include brake pads, T-screws, nuts, and neoprene bearings—everything needed for a shop-day rebuild rather than a full spindle replacement.

Plan spindle rebuilds around your slow season, not around failure. A rebuilt spindle installed in the winter costs a shop day; a spindle that fails mid-shift on a highway project costs a full day of production plus emergency shipping.

Seasonal: Upgrade Kit Windows

The end of your busy season is the right time to evaluate whether upgrade kits make sense for the coming year. GAC has shipped several rounds of improvements between 2019 and 2024 that drop into existing telescoping and bolt-together machines with minimal fabrication.

  • Four-position articulating center brush mount: better crown-following on modern crowned roads
  • Laser-cut multi-bar tension plates: tighter tolerance on slider positions and pin angles
  • Roller module for grids and composites: handles stiffer materials that older PVC bars struggle with
  • Heavier shafts and forged hubs: extends spindle life on high-tonnage operations
  • Hydraulic swing-arm conversions: replaces mechanical crab-claw arms for on-the-fly adjustment
  • Polypropylene extra-fill brushes: higher melt rating and 30 percent more filament coverage per block

Send the machine's serial number to GAC and the team will match upgrade kits to your build year. Most kits ship as job-ready packages with labeled hardware bags, so a shop can complete the installation in a half day.

Recordkeeping That Actually Gets Used

Maintenance schedules only work if someone remembers what happened last time. A simple logbook—physical or digital—that tracks daily walk-around notes, brush block swaps, spindle rebuilds, and upgrade installations pays for itself the first time a new operator asks "when was this greased last?"

Attach the log to the machine, not to a shop office. It'll get used.

The "99 Percent Still in Service" Standard

The reason nearly every Grizzly ever built is still running isn't luck—it's the combination of overbuilt engineering and operators who took maintenance seriously. Neither one works alone. A great machine that gets neglected fails eventually. A well-maintained machine that was cheap to begin with fails sooner.

Follow the schedule, order consumables in advance, plan upgrades around your slow season, and the Grizzly will outlast the tractor it's mounted to. That's the standard the platform was designed for.
